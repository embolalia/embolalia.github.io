tl;dr: The pope once issued an edict granting control of the Americas to the Spanish and Portuguese. What if the Man in the Moon issued a similar edict, granting control of Earth to aliens from the moon? What if these Lunatics considered us nothing more than savages, because their technology is more advanced? What if they saw us as inferior because we have a hideous white complexion, as opposed to their beautiful pea-green? What if they forced us to convert from our heathen Christianity to their Lunatic philosophy and way of life? What if they forced us out of all the good lands, to be confined to the tundras and deserts? Is this how we would want to be treated? It's how we treated the natives here, so obviously the answer is yes.

---

I really like this passage from *A History of New York* but, like the rest of the book, it's in a very dated style and thus challenging to read. I decided to spend some time modernizing it a bit. I swapped out some obscure words and simplified the complex sentence structures. Some meaning and beauty are, of course, lost in this, but I think it's worth it to make it more readable. You can always choose to read [the original](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Knickerbocker%27s_History_of_New_York/Book_I/Chapter_V) (`^F` Pope Alexander for where I started) instead.

---

In case there's any guilt about the matter, and to settle the question of whether the colonists had any right to settle, Pope Alexander Ⅵ issued an edict. With it he generously granted the newly-discovered quarter of the globe to the Spanish and Portuguese. With law and religion on their side, and being fired up by spiritual zeal, they showed the pagan savages neither favor nor affection. Instead, they carried out the work of discovery, colonization, civilization, and extermination with ten times more fury than ever.

Thus, the Europeans who first discovered America were clearly entitled to the soil. Likewise, they were entitled to the eternal thanks of these infidel savages. Europeans came so far, endured so much, and took great pains, for no other reason than to improve their forlorn, uncivilized, and heathenish condition. Europeans deserved thanks for introducing them the comforts of life, for showing them to the light of religion, and finally, for having hurried them out of the world to enjoy its reward!

But we selfish mortals never understand an argument as well as when it is related to ourselves. I want to put this question to rest forever, so I will suppose a parallel case in order to capture the attention my my readers.

Let's suppose that there are people who live on the moon. Let's suppose that, through astonishing advancement in science, they developed such command of physics that they were able to navigate space. Let's suppose that a crew of them chose to land on this planet. Here I beg my readers not to smile, as is often the case when readers peruse the grave speculations of philosophers. I am not being playful in this supposition, nor is it as wild a supposition as many may consider it. This has long been a very serious and anxious question for me. In my overwhelming care for this planet, I have often had sleepless nights debating in my mind whether it's most probable that we will first discover and civilize the moon, or the moon will discover and civilize us. Sailing in the air or cruising among the stars would be no more astonishing to us than the European capability of navigating floating castles through the world of waters would have been to the simple savages. We have already discovered the art of coasting through the aerial shores of our planet by means of balloons, as the savages had discovered the art of traveling along their sea coasts in canoes. The disparity between our balloons and the vessels of the people from the moon might not be greater than the disparity between the canoes of the savages and the mighty ships of Europeans. I could go on in an endless chain of similar speculations, but they aren't important to what I'm saying, so I leave them to the reader as matters worthy of consideration.

Going back to my supposition, let's suppose that these visitors from space, riding on hippogriffs, with their impenetrable armor and weapons that fire concentrated sunbeams, know far more than us. Let's suppose that they are as superior to us in knowledge (and therefore power) as the Europeans were to the Indians when they first discovered them. This is very possible; it's only our arrogance that makes us think differently. Before white men arrived, with our steel and gunpowder, I think the savages were as perfectly convinced that they themselves were the wisest, most virtuous, powerful, and perfect people as the people of England, France, or even America are now.

Let's suppose that these aliens find this planet to be nothing but wilderness, inhabited by us poor savages and wild beasts. Let's suppose they take formal possession of it, in the name of his most gracious and philosophic excellency, the Man in the Moon. But finding that they don't have enough people to control it completely, because of the ferocious barbarity of its inhabitants, they take our President, the King of England, the Emperor of Haiti, the mighty Bonaparte, and the great King of Bantam[^1] and, returning to the moon, take them to their court, as Indian chiefs were taken as spectacles in the courts of Europe.

[^1]: A region of what's now Indonesia

Then, following the etiquette of the court, they will address the powerful Man in the Moon in roughly these terms:

"Most serene and mighty ruler, whose territories extend as far as the eyes can reach, who rides on the great bear Ursa Major, uses the sun as a mirror, and controls the tides, madmen, and sea crabs. We, your subjects, have just returned from a voyage of discovery. While on it, we have landed on and taken possession of that obscure little dirty planet which you see in the sky. The five monsters we have brought with us were once very important chiefs among their fellow savages. These savages are a race of beings who lack the common attributes of humanity, and differ in every respect from the inhabitants of the moon. They carry their heads upon their shoulders, instead of under their arms, have two eyes instead of one, and completely lack tails. They are of a variety of ugly complexions, particularly a horrible whiteness, instead of pea-green.

"We found these miserable savages in a state of utter ignorance and depravity. Every man lives shamelessly with his own wife and rears his own children, instead of being part of the community of wives expected by the law of nature, as set out by the philosophers of the moon. They lack even a gleam of true philosophy among them, but are, in fact, utter heretics, ignoramuses, and barbarians. So we took compassion on the sad condition of the sublunary wretches and tried, while we were on their planet, to introduce them to the light of reason and the comforts of the moon. We treated them to mouthfuls of moonshine and hits of nitrous oxide, which they—particularly the females—swallowed with great voracity. We also tried to teach them the principals of lunar philosophy. We have insisted that they renounce the contemptible shackles of religion and common sense, and instead accept the profound, omnipotent, and all perfect energy, and the ecstatic immutable, immovable perfection. But these wretched savages were so incredibly stubborn that they continued staying with their wives and adhering to their religion, and treated the sublime doctrines of the moon as worthless. Among other terrible heresies they even went so far as to blasphemously declare that the moon was made of nothing more or less than green cheese!"

At this, the great Man in the Moon will become enraged and, possessing equal authority over things that do not belong to him as the Pope once did, will issue an edict:

"A crew of Lunatics have discovered and taken possession of a newly-discovered planet called the earth. It is inhabited by nothing but a race of two-legged animals that carry their heads on their shoulders instead of under their arms, cannot talk the Lunatic language, have two eyes instead of one, completely lack tails, and are of a horrible whiteness instead of pea-green. For this, and for a variety of other excellent reasons, they are considered incapable of possessing any property in the planet they infest, and the right and title to it are confirmed to its original discoverers. Furthermore, the colonists who are now about to leave for that planet are authorized and commanded to use every means to convert these infidel savages from the darkness of Christianity, and make them thorough and absolute Lunatics."

Following this edict, the aliens zealously go to work. They seize all our best land, take all of our belongings, and separate us from our wives. When we unreasonably complain, they will turn to us and say, "Miserable barbarians! Ungrateful wretches! Have we not come thousands of miles to improve your worthless planet? Have we not fed you with moonshine! Have we not intoxicated you with nitrous oxide? Doesn't our moon give you light every night? And you dare to complain when we take something in exchange for it?" But when they see that not only do we still have contempt for their reasoning and disbelieve in their philosophy, but even daringly defend our property, their patience will be exhausted. They will resort to their superior powers of argument—hunt us with hippogriffs, pierce us with concentrated sunbeams, demolish our cities with moonstones—until they have forced us to convert to the true faith. They will graciously permit us to exist in the torrid deserts of Arabia, or the frozen regions of northern Scandinavia. There we will enjoy the blessings of civilization and the charms of lunar philosophy, much like how the reformed and enlightened savages of this country are kindly suffered to inhabit the inhospitable forests of the north, or the impenetrable wilderness of South America.

I hope this has clearly proved, and strikingly illustrated, the right of the early colonists to the possession of this country. As such, this gigantic question is completely resolved. So, having bravely overcome all obstacles, and subdued all opposition, what's left but to bring my readers into the city which we have so long been, in a way, besieging. But wait: before I take another step I must pause to catch my breath, and recover from the excessive fatigue I have been through in preparing to begin this most accurate of histories. I do this by imitating the example of a renowned Dutch acrobat from antiquity, who took a start of three miles to jump over a hill. Having run himself out of breath by the time he reached the foot, he sat down quietly for a few moments to catch his breath, and then walked over it at his leisure.
